## 💬 Idioms

- *Adding a splash of dashi to the eggs was a total game changer — they turned out so much richer.*
- *That Japanese restaurant owner's tip was a game changer for my weekend breakfasts.*

### “bring out the flavor”

to enhance or intensify the natural taste of an ingredient

- *A pinch of salt in the egg mixture really brings out the flavor.*

## 📚 Vocabulary to Remember

- **silky** — smooth, soft, and slightly glossy in texture — often used to describe perfectly cooked eggs or sauces
  - *The eggs had a silky texture that melted in my mouth.*
- **umami** — a rich, savory taste considered the fifth basic taste alongside sweet, sour, salty, and bitter
  - *Adding a little soy sauce gave the dish a deep umami flavor.*
- **technique** — a specific, practiced method or skill used to achieve a particular result
  - *The folding technique is what gives tamagoyaki its layered shape.*
- **restaurant-quality** — as good as food prepared by a professional chef in a restaurant
  - *With the right technique, you can make restaurant-quality eggs at home.*
- **deceptively simple** — appearing easy or basic, but actually requiring care, skill, or close attention to detail
  - *Scrambled eggs are deceptively simple — small changes in heat make a huge difference.*
- **seasoning** — salt, spices, or other flavorings added to food to enhance its taste
  - *The chef's seasoning was subtle but made the eggs taste far more complex.*
- **whisk** — to beat eggs or a liquid mixture rapidly using a fork or a whisk tool
  - *Whisk the eggs thoroughly before pouring them into the pan.*
- **custard-like** — having a smooth, creamy, and softly set texture similar to custard
  - *The tamago had a custard-like consistency that was barely set and incredibly tender.*

## 🔧 Say It Naturally

1. ❌ *I didn't know eggs can taste so good like this.*
   ✅ **I had no idea eggs could taste this good.**
   💡 'Had no idea' conveys genuine, belated surprise; mixing past and present tenses as in the learner version sounds grammatically off.
2. ❌ *The secret is to cook them with low heat in a slow way.*
   ✅ **The trick is to cook them low and slow.**
   💡 'Low and slow' is a fixed cooking expression; unpacking it into separate phrases sounds awkward and wordy.
3. ❌ *It seems simple but there is technique inside it.*
   ✅ **It looks simple, but there's a real technique to it.**
   💡 English uses 'there's a technique to it' — the preposition 'to' links the skill to the task; 'inside it' doesn't work here.
4. ❌ *After you try in this way, you will never return to before.*
   ✅ **Once you try it this way, you'll never go back.**
   💡 'Go back' is the natural choice; 'return to before' is a literal translation that sounds unnatural in English.
5. ❌ *Try it — you will not get disappointment.*
   ✅ **Give it a try — you won't be disappointed.**
   💡 Use the adjective 'disappointed' to describe the person's feeling, not the noun 'disappointment' as a direct object.
6. ❌ *I was making eggs in wrong way all my life.*
   ✅ **I've been making eggs wrong my whole life.**
   💡 'Wrong' works as an adverb directly after the verb; you also need 'the wrong way,' not 'in wrong way,' if you use that structure.
7. ❌ *A small amount of seasoning is very effective and enough.*
   ✅ **A little seasoning goes a long way.**
   💡 'Goes a long way' is a natural idiom meaning a small amount produces a big effect; spelling it out sounds flat and clunky.
8. ❌ *The restaurant owner is very skillful at their job.*
   ✅ **The restaurant owner really knows their craft.**
   💡 'Knows their craft' is a natural, respectful phrase for a skilled professional with deep mastery; it carries more admiration than 'skillful at their job.'
