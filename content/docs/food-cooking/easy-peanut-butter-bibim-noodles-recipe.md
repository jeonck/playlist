## 💬 Idioms

### “a must”

something absolutely necessary or essential that should not be skipped

- *Good waterproof shoes are a must if you plan to hike in the rain.*
- *When making bibim noodles, vinegar is a must — it brightens the whole dish.*

### “to your taste”

adjusted according to your own personal preference or liking

- *Add as much chili as you like — season the dish to your taste.*
- *The recipe is just a guide; feel free to change the sweetness to your taste.*

### “have something at home”

to already own an ingredient or item in your house so you use it instead of buying something else

- *She used soba noodles because she had them at home and didn't want to go to the store.*
- *I made fried rice with whatever vegetables I had at home.*

## 📚 Vocabulary to Remember

- **seasoning** — a mixture of spices, sauces, or other ingredients added to food to give it flavor
  - *She mixed soy sauce, garlic, and sugar to make the seasoning for the salad.*
- **transfer** — to move something carefully from one container or place to another
  - *Once the pasta is ready, transfer it to a large serving bowl before adding the sauce.*
- **sprinkle** — to scatter a small amount of something lightly over a surface
  - *He sprinkled a handful of chopped herbs over the soup just before serving.*
- **sticky** — tending to cling or stick together, not separating easily
  - *The rice became sticky after sitting in the pot with the lid on for too long.*
- **vinegar** — a sharp, sour liquid used to season food or add a tangy kick, common in salads and Asian dishes
  - *A splash of vinegar in the dipping sauce gave it a pleasant tang.*
- **sesame oil** — a fragrant, nutty oil pressed from sesame seeds, widely used to finish East and Southeast Asian dishes
  - *Drizzle a little sesame oil over the noodles at the end for extra aroma.*

## 🔧 Say It Naturally

1. ❌ *Vinegar is very important. It becomes more delicious if you add vinegar.*
   ✅ **Vinegar is a must — it tastes much better with vinegar.**
   💡 'A must' strongly signals something essential in one word; following it with a direct comparison in the same breath sounds confident and natural in spoken English.
2. ❌ *Eat it in the way that you personally prefer.*
   ✅ **Eat it to your taste.**
   💡 'To your taste' is a compact expression that invites personal adjustment without listing every option — much smoother than a long explanation.
3. ❌ *I used rice noodles because I already had rice noodles inside my house.*
   ✅ **I used rice noodles because I had them at home.**
   💡 'Had them at home' avoids repeating the noun and uses the idiomatic 'at home'; 'inside my house' sounds overly formal and literal.
4. ❌ *It finished cooking, so you should move it to the plate now.*
   ✅ **It's well cooked, so transfer it to a plate.**
   💡 'Transfer to a plate' is the standard kitchen instruction; 'move it to the plate' is understood but sounds less fluent in a cooking context.
5. ❌ *Put the sauce that I made before on top, and put green onion and sesame seeds on it.*
   ✅ **Pour the sauce I made earlier over it and sprinkle green onion and sesame seeds.**
   💡 'Pour over' and 'sprinkle' are precise cooking verbs; the vague 'put … on' repeated twice sounds flat and doesn't show the reader how to do each action.
6. ❌ *The noodles were sticking together too much, so I put some water into them.*
   ✅ **The noodles were a little sticky, so I added a little water.**
   💡 The adjective 'sticky' captures the texture in one word; 'put some water into them' is awkward — 'added a little water' is the natural fix.
7. ❌ *It has a good smell, and the color looks nice.*
   ✅ **It smells good, and the color is really pretty.**
   💡 Using 'smells good' (subject + linking verb + adjective) is more natural than 'has a good smell' (noun phrase); native speakers default to the linking-verb form in casual speech.
